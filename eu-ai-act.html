The EU AI Act on One Page

My study notes while preparing for the IAPP AIGP certification · Updated October 2026

What it is

The EU AI Act is the European Union's law on artificial intelligence. It entered into force on 1 August 2024. It applies to companies that build, sell or use AI systems in the EU, including companies based outside the EU whose AI is used there.

The four risk levels

LevelExamplesWhat the law requires
UnacceptableSocial scoring, manipulative AI that exploits vulnerable people, most real-time facial recognition in public by policeBanned
High riskAI used in hiring, credit decisions, education, critical infrastructure, and safety parts of regulated productsRisk management, data quality, documentation, human oversight, logging, accuracy and security
Limited riskChatbots, AI-generated images, video and textTransparency: tell people they're dealing with AI, and label AI-generated content
Minimal riskSpam filters, AI in video gamesNo new requirements

Separate rules cover general-purpose AI models (large models that power many applications), including technical documentation and copyright policies. The most powerful models carry extra duties.

Key dates

In July 2026 the EU adopted the Digital Omnibus on AI, which moved the high-risk deadlines back.

DateWhat applies
2 Feb 2025Bans on unacceptable-risk AI
2 Aug 2025Rules for general-purpose AI models
2 Aug 2026Transparency duties for chatbots and AI-generated content
2 Dec 2026New bans on AI that creates non-consensual intimate images or child sexual abuse material (added by the Omnibus)
2 Dec 2027High-risk AI used in areas like hiring, credit and education (was 2 Aug 2026)
2 Aug 2028High-risk AI built into regulated products such as medical devices and machinery
2 Aug 2030High-risk AI used by public authorities

Fines

  • Banned AI: up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher
  • Most other violations: up to €15 million or 3%
  • Giving regulators wrong information: up to €7.5 million or 1%

What a company should do now

  1. Make an inventory of every AI system you build or use.
  2. Sort each system into a risk level.
  3. Meet the transparency rules that already apply.
  4. For high-risk systems, use the extra time to build risk management, documentation and human oversight.
  5. Put someone in charge of AI governance.

My AIGP study notes: all four domains

The IAPP's AI Governance Professional (AIGP) certification covers four domains. The EU AI Act above is one part of Domain 2. These are my one-section summaries of each domain.

Domain 1 · Understanding the Foundations of AI Governance

The base layer: what AI is and how it learns, how it can cause harm, the ethics vocabulary, and the first steps to build a governance program.

  • AI is a family of techniques. Machine learning (systems that learn patterns from data rather than fixed rules) sits inside AI, with deep learning and generative AI nested further in. Learning styles: supervised (labeled examples), unsupervised (unlabeled data), semi-supervised (a mix) and reinforcement (learning by reward).
  • Models come in many sizes. Foundation models are huge networks reused or fine-tuned (given extra training) for many jobs. Small language models trade breadth for lower cost, speed and the option to run on a company's own servers.
  • AI runs on a tech stack. Picture four layers: compute (processing power, such as GPU chips), storage, network and software on top.
  • Harms reach every level. Individuals face bias and privacy loss; society faces disinformation, deepfakes (faked media) and echo chambers; companies face reputational, financial, legal and intellectual property harm. Misalignment, where an AI pursues a goal other than the intended one, is a key root cause.
  • Why AI needs its own governance. AI can be complex and opaque (a "black box" whose reasoning you cannot see), act on its own, work fast and at scale, depend on data quality, and give varying answers to the same input.
  • Ethics terms. Ethical AI asks whether to build something, responsible AI puts values into practice, and trustworthy AI is the measurable result. Core ideas include fairness, transparency, accountability and contestability (the ability to challenge a decision).
  • Building the program. Know your role, tailor governance to size, maturity, industry and risk tolerance, assess use cases with the NIST (U.S. standards agency) map, measure, manage steps, involve stakeholders, train staff in AI literacy, and build a responsible culture.

Domain 2 · AI Laws, Standards, and Frameworks

This domain is about the rulebook for AI: which laws already apply, what the EU AI Act requires, how the US and other countries handle AI, and which voluntary frameworks help an organization put good practice into action.

  • Three ways to regulate. Countries either pass one broad AI law (the EU, South Korea), write rules for specific areas such as hiring or banking, or update laws they already have (Brazil). Because these approaches clash, a global company should build one internal program around the strictest rules it faces.
  • Old laws still apply. Privacy principles, Privacy by Design, the GDPR (the EU's data protection law), and intellectual property rules all reach AI. In the US, agencies such as the EEOC (job discrimination) and the FTC (consumer protection) enforce existing laws against harmful AI uses.
  • EU AI Act risk levels. The Act puts AI into four tiers: banned, high risk (strict mandatory duties), limited risk (people must be told they are dealing with AI), and minimal risk (voluntary). Risk means how bad a harm could be combined with how likely it is.
  • Six operator roles. Duties depend on your role: provider, deployer, importer, distributor, product manufacturer, or authorized representative. Providers, who build the system, carry the heaviest load, and one company can hold several roles.
  • General-purpose models and enforcement. Large foundation models have their own chapter of rules, with extra duties when their training compute is very high. National market surveillance bodies and the EU AI Office enforce the Act and can impose fines.
  • Frameworks show the how. Laws say what to do; standards explain how. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is built on four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. ISO/IEC 42001 sets up a certifiable AI management system.
  • 2026 changes. The EU pushed high-risk deadlines to late 2027 and 2028 and banned apps that make non-consensual intimate images. US states keep copying four model law types: frontier safety, consequential decisions, transparency, and chatbot interaction.

Domain 3 · Governing AI Development

This domain follows an AI system from the first idea to the point where it is ready to launch. It covers planning, judging risk, handling data, building the model, and proving through testing and documentation that the system is safe and does what it should.

  • A looping life cycle. An AI system moves through seven stages: plan and design, collect and prepare data, build or select the model, test it, deploy it, monitor it, and finally retire it. Teams often circle back to earlier stages when something changes, so the stages repeat instead of running once in a straight line.
  • Plan before you build. Start by defining the business problem and asking whether AI is the right answer at all. Then set the scope, check that usable data exists, name a senior leader as the project's champion, and decide who owns oversight, appeals, and the "kill switch" (a way to shut the system off).
  • Scoring risk. A simple risk score multiplies how likely a harm is by how bad it would be. To reduce risk, removing the hazard entirely works best, and paperwork-style controls are the weakest option.
  • Data quality and splits. Good data is correct, complete, current, representative, and checked for bias. Data is usually split into training, validation, and test sets, roughly 60/20/20. A model that memorizes its training data and fails on new data is "overfitting."
  • Protecting privacy. Privacy enhancing technologies let teams use data while exposing less about people. Examples include federated learning (training without pooling everyone's data in one place) and differential privacy (adding random noise to hide individuals).
  • Assurance and oversight. Audits, certifications such as ISO 42001, and EU conformity assessments for high-risk systems show that rules are being followed. A DPIA (data protection impact assessment) is the GDPR review for systems that use personal data.
  • Documenting for others. Model cards work like nutrition labels for a model, and datasheets explain why a dataset was made, what is in it, how it was gathered, and what it is meant for. Red teaming means attacking your own system on purpose to find weak spots before others do.

Domain 4 · Governing AI Deployment and Use

This domain covers what happens once an AI system leaves the lab: getting it ready for launch, buying AI from vendors, watching it while it runs, handling things when they go wrong, and switching it off safely at the end.

  • Check before launch. Before go-live, find out who built the model, whether someone fine-tuned it (gave it extra training for one job), and who will use it. Then review policies on privacy, security, ownership of outputs, engineering practice and open source, and run a readiness check on function, performance, compliance and documentation.
  • Deployment choices. A system can run in the cloud, on the company's own servers, or on "edge" devices like phones. Each choice trades off things like scaling, control, up-front cost, delay and privacy. Software is often packed into containers, bundles that carry the code and everything it needs.
  • AI agents need extra guardrails. Agents act on their own across many steps, so they need human review, a kill switch (a way to shut them down fast), and safe default behavior. One key rule is to avoid giving an agent personal data, untrusted content and outside communication all at once.
  • Vendor and proprietary models. With a closed model you cannot see its code, design or learned settings. So contracts should spell out data use, testing results, bias controls, who owns outputs, and whether the vendor covers you if someone sues over intellectual property.
  • Ongoing monitoring. Keep a list of every AI system, give each a risk score that guides how much attention it gets, and watch for data drift, meaning real-world data slowly moving away from the training data. Red teaming (simulated attacks), stress tests (pushing the system to extremes) and "challenger" models (a new model tested against the current one) help find weaknesses.
  • Incident response. Be ready for six types of failure, including discrimination, privacy and physical safety failures. Respond in six steps: prepare, identify, contain, eradicate, recover and learn lessons.
  • Disclosure, audits and retirement. People often must be told when AI affects them, and audits (some of them automated) support accountability. When a system is retired, an AI bill of materials, a full list of its parts, helps make sure data and models are kept or destroyed properly.
