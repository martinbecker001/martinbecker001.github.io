What I Learned Assessing Four AI Systems for Risk

Six lessons from building an AI governance program

As part of an AI governance program I built, I ran full risk assessments on four AI systems. They ranged from an assistant that answers questions to tools that can take actions on their own. I used the method on this page. Here's what I'd tell anyone starting the same work.

1. Start with an inventory, because you can't govern what you can't find

Before assessing anything, I built a list of every AI system. The information was spread across many documents, teams and tools. That inventory became the foundation for everything else.

2. Use frameworks people already trust

I built the method on the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 23894 and the OWASP Top 10 lists. Engineers, auditors and customers already know these frameworks, so I spent less time defending the method and more time on the findings.

3. What the AI can do matters more than what it can say

The biggest driver of risk was autonomy. A system that only answers questions is one thing. A system that can run code, change records or call other tools needs much tighter permissions, logging and human review.

4. Your vendor's model is part of your risk

Most AI systems are built on a model from an outside provider. Each assessment needs to cover the provider's data handling, retention and security, not just your own code.

5. Ask for evidence, not just answers

A self-assessment says "yes, we do that." A second-line review asks "show me." Requiring evidence such as screenshots, test results or configuration made the results trustworthy and found gaps that a questionnaire would have missed.

6. A finding without an owner and a date is just an opinion

Every finding went into a remediation tracker with a severity, an owner and a deadline based on severity (30, 60 or 90 days). The governance board reviews that tracker, and the tracker is what turns an assessment into actual risk reduction.
